More than one GPU

You'll be able to say when a second GPU makes a job faster and when it makes it slower, with arithmetic you can do by hand. Your compiler needs this picture because this week it learns to split one program across several GPUs, and every rule it follows comes from one fact: GPUs do not share memory.

Two GPUs, twice as fast?

People use several GPUs for two reasons. A job can be too big: the model and its data do not fit in one GPU's memory. Or it can be too slow: training on one GPU would take weeks. Either way, the hope is that two GPUs do the job in half the time. Guess first.

A job takes 8 seconds on one GPU. You split the work evenly over two GPUs. How long does it take now?

The data starts on the first GPU. What has to happen before the second GPU can start?

More than 4. Each GPU does 4 seconds of work, but first half the data has to travel to the second GPU, and afterward its answers have to travel back. Those copies take time that one GPU never spent.

The work really does split in half. The extra time is the copying. Whether a second GPU helps comes down to one comparison: is the time saved on work bigger than the time spent on copies? The rest of this lesson builds the picture behind that question and then the arithmetic to answer it.

Each GPU has its own memory

From now on a GPU in a program is called a device, numbered from 0. Each device has its own global memory, and a kernel running on one device can only read and write that device's memory. It cannot see another device's memory at all.

So anything a device needs must be copied into its memory first. A copy goes over the wire between the GPUs, which is far slower than a GPU reading its own memory. Step through it.

A buffer holds 1,000,000 floats of 4 bytes each, so 4 MB. It sits on device 0. Device 1 needs the second half. How many megabytes must be copied to device 1?

Half of 4 MB.

2 MB. Device 1 gets only the part it uses. Copying all 4 MB would work too, but it would take twice as long.

Five words for moving memory

The whole week uses five words. Here they are one at a time, on a small job. A lookup table t holds 4 numbers. For each element of x, the job writes o[i] = t[x[i]]: the table entry that x[i] names. Two devices share the work, four elements each.

A shard is the same idea as a thread's slice in lesson 7.4, with a device in place of the thread: device d owns elements d·S to d·S + S - 1. Lesson 10.2 brings that rule back word for word.

Now four devices share the same job. x has 8 elements and is sharded. t has 4 elements and is replicated. How many elements does the scatter copy to device 1?

Device 1's shard of x is 8 ÷ 4 elements. It also needs all of t.

2 + 4 = 6. Notice the difference: with more devices each shard of x gets smaller, but every device still needs all 4 elements of t. Sharding splits the copying. Replicating multiplies it.

What decides if more GPUs help

Copying costs the same per byte whatever the job does with the bytes. So the question that decides everything is: how much work does the job do for each byte it moves? Compare two jobs.

Which job gains more from a second GPU?

How many times is each number used once it arrives?

The matrix multiply. Doubling uses each number once, so the GPU finishes it faster than the wire can deliver it. In an 8192×8192 matrix multiply, every number is used 8192 times, so each byte moved pays for a lot of work.

A cost model you can compute by hand

A cost model is a small formula that predicts how long something takes. Ours has two parts. The work splits evenly over the devices. The copies do not split: they go one after another over the same wire, so their time is the data moved divided by the copy speed.

time on 1 device  = work
time on N devices = work / N  +  data moved / copy speed

On one device the data is already where it needs to be, so nothing is copied. The speedup is the time on one device divided by the time on N. Take the 8 second job, which moves 4 GB in all to and from the second device, with copies at 4 GB per second. These numbers are illustrative too.

  1. Split the work. 8 / 2 = 4 seconds.
  2. Find the copy time. 4 GB / 4 GB per second = 1 second.
  3. Add them. 4 + 1 = 5 seconds. The speedup is 8 / 5 = 1.6, not 2.

The same job runs on 4 devices. All its buffers are sharded, so the shards still add up to 4 GB moved. How many seconds does it take?

Split the work 4 ways, then add the copy time, which did not change.

8 / 4 + 1 = 3 seconds, a speedup of about 2.7 on 4 devices. The work keeps shrinking, the copy time does not. That is why adding devices helps less and less.

Fix the cost model

time_on takes the number of devices n, the work on one device in milliseconds, the data moved mb in megabytes and the copy speed in megabytes per millisecond. It returns the time in whole milliseconds, using whole-number division. One device copies nothing. The starter gets 2 or more devices wrong: it splits the copy time over the devices too. When a second GPU makes it slower

A short job does little work, but its data still has to move. Predict this one.

A job takes 1 second on one GPU. On two, it moves 6 GB at 4 GB per second. How long does it take on two?

Work over 2, plus data over speed.

1 / 2 + 6 / 4 = 0.5 + 1.5 = 2 seconds. Twice as slow as one GPU. The copies cost more than the second GPU saved.

Two devices win when work / 2 + copy < work, where copy is the copy time. Take work / 2 from both sides: they win when half the work is more than the copy time. With N devices the saving is bigger, work - work / N, but the copy still has to fit under it. To find the smallest job that wins without fractions, multiply both sides by N:

work + N · copy  <  N · work

Check it on the job above: 1 + 2 · 1.5 = 4, which is not less than 2 · 1 = 2. Two devices lose, as you found.

The smallest job worth splitting

Write smallest_work. It takes the number of devices n (at least 2) and the copy time copy in whole milliseconds. It returns the smallest whole number of milliseconds of work for which n devices are strictly faster than one, using the inequality above.

What the cost model leaves out, for when real numbers disagree with it
  • Device 0 copies too. In this week's design the program's buffers live on device 0, and the scatter also gives device 0 its own copy of its shard. That copy stays inside one GPU, which is far faster than the wire, so the model leaves it out. The # cuda copy: budget in the tests does count it.
  • Copies can overlap. Real systems copy to several devices at once over separate wires, and copy the next piece while computing the current one. Then the copy time can partly hide behind the work. This week's compiler does neither, so the simple sum is close to what it does.
  • Wires differ. GPUs joined through the main board (PCIe) copy at tens of GB per second. GPUs joined by a direct link (NVLink on NVIDIA cards) copy at hundreds. The numbers in this lesson are illustrative only.
  • Not every part splits. Some work, such as adding up the partial results of each device, stays on one device. Lessons 10.4 and 10.5 show it.
  • Copies are not free on one GPU either. The harness copies buffers from the CPU to the GPU and back, as in week 8. Both setups pay that, so the model leaves it out of the comparison.
The too-big reason, with numbers

A model with 7 billion weights stored as 4-byte floats needs 28 GB just for the weights, before any activations or gradients. A GPU with 16 GB of memory cannot hold it. Sharding the weights over two such GPUs gives each 14 GB, which fits. For a job like that a second GPU is not about speed at all: without it the job cannot run. These sizes are illustrative, and real systems also store weights in 2-byte or smaller formats.

Check yourself

What does a gather do?

Scatter sends out. Gather is the other direction.

A gather brings the results home. (a) is a scatter of shards, and (c) is replicating.

A 100 MB table is replicated to 4 devices, counting device 0's own copy. How many megabytes does the scatter copy for it in all?

Every device gets the whole table.

4 · 100 = 400 MB. A sharded 100 MB buffer would move 100 MB in all, however many devices there are.

Every device reads only its own piece of a buffer. Should the buffer be sharded or replicated?

Which choice moves fewer bytes?

Sharded. Replicating would give the same results but copy N times as much. Lesson 10.3 shows how the tests tell the two apart: they count the bytes.

In the cost model, why can't 2 devices ever take less than half the time of one?

Look at the two parts of work / N + data / speed.

The work part is exactly half. The copy part is zero or more. So the total is half or more, and equal to half only when nothing moves.
